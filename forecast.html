Australia 2030: The Agentic Economy

How AI agents will reshape Australian productivity, work and the structure of firms by 2030.

The Forecast

By 2030, AI agents are likely to reshape the Australian economy less through the wholesale replacement of jobs than through the automation of bundles of cognitive tasks. As AI systems become capable of planning, using tools and completing multi-step work with less continuous human direction, the effective cost of some forms of knowledge work will fall.

The economic gains will not be automatic or evenly distributed. Firms that combine agentic AI with redesigned workflows, complementary human skills and organisational change are likely to capture the largest productivity benefits, while workers whose tasks are easier to substitute may face greater adjustment pressure.

Australia is already moving along this path. ABS data show that 12% of Australian businesses reported using AI in 2024–25, compared with just 1% in 2022–23. This forecast argues that the central question for 2030 is therefore not simply whether AI becomes more capable, but whether Australian firms, workers and institutions adapt quickly enough to turn those capabilities into broadly shared productivity gains.

From Chatbots to Economic Agents

The economic significance of AI agents comes from action, not simply intelligence. A conventional chatbot responds to a prompt and stops. An agentic system can instead pursue a goal across multiple steps: it can gather information, make decisions, use software tools, observe the result of its actions and continue working with limited human direction.

This changes the unit of automation. Generative AI can substitute for individual tasks such as drafting text or summarising information; an agent can potentially automate a larger workflow made up of many connected tasks. In knowledge-intensive firms, this could include researching an issue, analysing documents, preparing a recommendation, updating records and communicating the result. By 2030, this shift from task assistance toward workflow execution is likely to be one of the main channels through which AI affects Australian firms.

Economically, AI agents can be understood as a form of capital that supplies labour-like cognitive services. Firms purchase or subscribe to the technology, but the output may replace effort that would previously have been purchased through wages. This makes agents a capital-labour hybrid: their services resemble labour, while the returns flow through ownership of software and productive capital. That distinction matters for productivity, firm structure and the distribution of the gains from automation.

When Intelligence Gets Cheaper

Productivity is the key channel connecting AI capability to living standards. This matters particularly for Australia, where productivity growth has been weak. Productivity Commission data show that labour productivity fell by 0.2% over the year to June 2026 and remained only around 1% above its 2015–19 average. Agentic AI therefore arrives at a time when Australia has a strong economic incentive to find new ways of producing more output from its existing labour and capital.

AI can be understood as a general-purpose technology: a technology capable of improving production across many industries rather than within a single narrow application. Agentic AI strengthens this mechanism because it can reduce the effective cost of complex cognitive workflows. If an employee supported by agents can complete more analysis, customer service, coding or administrative work in the same number of hours, labour productivity rises. If agents execute some workflows independently, firms may also expand output without increasing headcount proportionally.

The scale of this effect remains uncertain. The Productivity Commission estimates that AI could increase Australian labour productivity by around 4.3% over the next decade and add approximately $116 billion to GDP, while noting that published estimates vary widely. This forecast therefore does not assume that technical capability automatically becomes economic growth. The baseline scenario assumes meaningful productivity gains by 2030, but only where firms combine better AI with adoption, complementary skills and redesigned workflows.

Jobs Will Change Before They Disappear

The labour-market effect of AI is better understood at the level of tasks than occupations. Most jobs contain bundles of activities, only some of which can be automated. AI can therefore create a displacement effect by performing tasks previously completed by workers, while also creating a complementarity effect when it makes workers more productive in the tasks that remain.

Australian evidence currently points more strongly toward augmentation than wholesale job replacement. Jobs and Skills Australia finds that generative AI is more likely to enhance workers' capabilities than eliminate entire occupations, although automation exposure is higher in routine forms of work. As agentic systems become more capable, the boundary may shift because agents can execute connected workflows rather than isolated tasks. Even then, occupations requiring judgement, accountability, interpersonal interaction and adaptation are likely to retain substantial human input.

The outcome by 2030 will therefore depend on the balance between displacement and reinstatement. Some existing tasks may disappear, but productivity growth can also expand output and create new tasks in supervision, verification, client interaction, AI integration and other complementary activities. The key risk is timing: if automation occurs faster than workers can acquire complementary skills or move into new tasks, Australia could experience significant adjustment pressures even without permanent mass unemployment.

Three Australias in 2030

The effect of AI agents on Australia by 2030 will depend on more than technical capability. The speed of business adoption, worker adaptation, organisational change, competition and public policy will determine whether productivity gains are broadly shared or concentrated. Three scenarios illustrate plausible trajectories rather than precise predictions.

Baseline: Managed Diffusion

AI agents spread steadily through professional services, finance, administration, technology and other knowledge-intensive industries. Firms automate selected workflows while keeping humans responsible for judgement, verification and client-facing decisions. Productivity improves, but adoption remains uneven because large firms have greater access to skills, data and implementation resources. Employment adjusts mainly through changing task composition rather than widespread job destruction. Wage gains favour workers who can effectively supervise and complement AI systems.

Upside: Broad Productivity Diffusion

Rapid capability improvements are matched by strong organisational adaptation. Small and medium-sized firms gain affordable access to agentic systems, workers acquire complementary skills and new tasks emerge quickly enough to offset much of the displacement effect. Competition remains strong and productivity gains spread beyond a small group of technology-intensive firms. In this scenario, AI raises output, supports new business formation and produces a broader increase in real incomes.

Downside: Concentrated Automation

AI capabilities improve faster than labour-market and institutional adaptation. Large firms use agents to automate increasingly complex workflows, but worker retraining and task creation lag behind. Productivity may still rise, yet a greater share of the gains flows to firms and owners of AI-related capital while exposed workers face weaker bargaining power and adjustment costs. Market concentration could also increase if access to advanced models, data and computing remains dominated by a small number of providers.

The critical difference between these scenarios is therefore not whether AI improves, but whether Australia develops the complementary skills, institutions and competitive conditions required to convert cheaper cognition into broadly distributed economic gains.
